1. Executive Summary
Study Topic Extractor is an AI/NLP-powered educational productivity platform that converts
unstructured study material (PDFs, lecture notes, textbook chapters, transcripts, plain text) into a
structured study roadmap: ranked topics, subtopics, keywords, short explanations, topic
relationships, and exam-focused revision output.
The product is not a keyword extractor. Keywords are one input. The output is a prioritized learning
map that answers one question: "What are the most important things I should study from this
document?"
The first release (MVP) uses classical, explainable NLP (spaCy, scikit-learn, TF-IDF, n-grams) with no
paid API dependency. Semantic embeddings, clustering, hierarchy, graph, Exam Focus Mode, question
generation, accounts and history follow in Phase 2. LLM assistance is optional and never required for
the core pipeline.
1.1 Document Set and Reading Order
This PRD is document 01 of 14. Terminology defined here (Topic, Keyword, Score, Priority, Analysis,
Study Roadmap) is used identically in every other document. Engineering agents should read
MASTER_PROJECT_CONTEXT.pdf first, then the document relevant to their task.

2. Product Vision
Every student should be able to turn hours of reading into a focused plan in under a minute. The
product should feel like an intelligent study-analysis platform: fast, trustworthy, explainable, and
pleasant to use on a phone the night before an exam.
  Tagline: Turn hours of reading into a focused study roadmap.
 Positioning: A general-purpose study analyzer. It is not tied to GATE, UPSC or any single exam.
 Principle: Explainable first. Every score can be traced to visible signals (frequency, distinctiveness,
position).

3. Problem Statement
Students receive large volumes of material, often hundreds of pages, and must decide what matters.
Manual topic extraction is slow, repetitive, inconsistent between students, and hardest for beginners
who lack the subject knowledge to judge importance.
3.1 Existing Problem
Current approach
Weakness
Reading everything linearly
Time-consuming; no prioritization; low retention
Manual highlighting / topic lists
Inconsistent; depends on prior knowledge; not reusable
Generic summarizers
Produce prose, not a ranked study map; no priority or relationships
Chatting with a general LLM
Non-deterministic, not exam-oriented, context-length limits, privacy
concerns, cost
Keyword extraction tools
Flat word lists with no hierarchy, priority, or explanations
3.2 Proposed Solution
A web application where the user pastes text or uploads a PDF and receives, within seconds, a ranked
list of topics with scores, HIGH/MEDIUM/LOW study priority, keywords, extractive summaries, and
export options. Phase 2 adds subtopic hierarchy, a relationship graph, Exam Focus Mode with possible
questions, saved history, and progress tracking.

4. Goals and Non-Goals
4.1 Product Goals
 1. G1. Produce a useful ranked topic list from a document in under 10 seconds for typical inputs.
2. G2. Make priority obvious (HIGH / MEDIUM / LOW) and understandable.
3. G3. Keep results explainable and deterministic for the same input and configuration.
4. G4. Protect private academic content (no public exposure, minimal retention).
5. G5. Be buildable by a student developer with free/open-source tools.
6. G6. Provide a clean, accessible, responsive experience in light and dark themes.
4.2 Non-Goals
  Payments, subscriptions, or social features (sharing, study rooms).
 Autonomous AI agents or a chat assistant in MVP or Phase 2.
 Full multilingual NLP in MVP (architecture must allow it later).
 Replacing textbooks: the product prioritizes, it does not teach.
 Guaranteeing that ranked topics equal what an examiner will ask. Output is decision support.

11. Constraints, Assumptions, Limitations
11.1 Product Constraints
  Free/open-source tooling; LLM APIs optional.
 Buildable by a student developer; no distributed architecture in MVP.
 English first; architecture must not block Hindi or Gujarati later.
 No payment or social features.
11.2 Assumptions
  Inputs are mostly English, text-based, academic material.
 Users accept that importance is inferred from document signals, not from an exam syllabus.
 Users have a modern browser; mobile Safari and Chrome are supported.
 Typical documents are 500 to 30,000 words.
11.3 Limitations
  Poorly written or very short notes reduce quality.
 TF-IDF alone cannot understand meaning; semantic layer arrives in Phase 2.
 Scanned PDFs need OCR (Phase 2). Diagrams and complex math notation are not analyzed.
 Ambiguous or domain-specific terminology may be missed.
 Very long unstructured documents lose precision without sectioning.
